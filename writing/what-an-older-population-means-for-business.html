What an Older Population Means for Business

Jacob George · November 2026

In 2024, Oji Holdings decided to stop making baby diapers in Japan and put more into its domestic adult diaper business. The company planned to keep its baby diaper business going in Malaysia and Indonesia. That decision shows what I find interesting about demographics. A company can be selling essentially the same kind of product in two parts of the world and face completely different prospects because its customers are at different stages of life. Over the next decade, I expect more businesses to rely on younger markets for sales growth while changing how they operate in countries where customers and workers are getting older.

The first thing I’d separate is the size of a population from its age. People are living longer, and lower birth rates mean that younger generations are smaller in many countries. An economy can start feeling the effects of aging while its total population is still growing. In Japan, people aged 65 or older made up 29.3% of the population in 2024. The UN puts Japan and Germany among the countries whose populations had already peaked by 2024, while it projects that India’s will keep growing through at least 2054. If I’m looking at where a company plans to sell a product, I’d want to know whether the age group that buys it is growing in that particular market.

There’s also a delay between falling birth rates and the change businesses eventually see in hiring. Derek Thompson’s discussion of the fertility decline looks at why it is happening across countries with different cultures and policies, while Noah Smith argues that governments have yet to find a reliable way to reverse it. Even a successful policy this year would take roughly two decades to affect the number of young adults entering the workforce. The OECD projects that the number of people aged 20 to 64 across its member countries will fall by 8% by 2060. That puts more pressure on the decisions companies can make now, including whether they can keep the workers who already know the job.

A smaller workforce can change what it makes sense to invest in. A company that can easily find people to fill shifts may put off buying equipment that would make each shift more productive. If it keeps losing orders because it cannot staff its second shift, the same purchase starts to make more sense. A factory could automate the movement of parts between stations so the people still on the floor can spend more time on work that requires judgment. By the mid-2030s, I expect companies in older economies to use labor-saving equipment for that kind of specific bottleneck, especially when the alternative is running existing facilities below capacity.

That choice looks different in jobs where time with a person is a large part of what customers are paying for. Japan’s health ministry estimates that the country will need about 2.72 million long-term care workers in fiscal year 2040, compared with about 2.15 million in 2022. A home care provider can use software to cut the time an aide spends driving, but the visit still needs someone there. If an agency can staff fewer appointments than families want, some families may end up doing more of the work themselves. That matters to other employers if workers reduce their hours to take care of a parent. It is one example of how a change in the age of customers can make the hiring problem worse at the same time.

I also expect companies to rethink what they ask of people near retirement. Japan’s Statistics Bureau found that people 65 and older accounted for 13.7% of employment in 2024, so the boundary between an older population and the workforce is already less clear than the age figures make it look. An experienced employee might be willing to stay an extra year if the company lets them work a shorter week or move into a role that is easier physically. That could be worth more to the employer than simply filling a vacancy, especially if the person knows how to train people who are new to the job. I’d expect that approach to matter most in businesses that take years to replace an experienced worker.

An older population also changes what governments have to pay for. The OECD projects the ratio of people 65 and older to people aged 20 to 64 will rise from about 31 per 100 in 2023 to 52 per 100 by 2060 across its member countries. Those figures describe age groups, so they include people over 65 who still work. Even so, they give a sense of why paying for pensions and public services could get harder if fewer people are working relative to the population. For a business that relies on public funding, I’d be careful about assuming that more people qualifying for a service will come with enough money to expand it.

The age of a market also changes the case for expanding there. Even if a country has more young people, a company still has to figure out what those customers can afford. A manufacturer that sells expensive appliances in Japan might find more first-time buyers in a younger country, but it may need to sell a simpler model at a lower price. Population growth gives a company more potential customers. Their budgets determine what it can sell them.

I expect the companies that handle this best to think about where they sell separately from how they get the work done. A manufacturer with an older customer base at home might expand a different product line abroad because buyers there are younger. A business that has to serve customers in person has less room to move the work, so I’d look more closely at how it keeps experienced staff and how much it can deliver per shift. If my prediction is right, by the mid-2030s we’ll see more companies putting money into capacity in younger markets while investing at home in ways to get more out of the workers they already have. The mistake would be to see a headline about a shrinking population and assume every company in the country is facing the same future.

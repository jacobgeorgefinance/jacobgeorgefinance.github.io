What an Older Population Means for Business

Jacob George · November 2026

A company can have more people waiting to buy its services and still struggle to grow because it cannot hire enough people to do the work. I think that will become more common as populations age. A home care agency might get calls from families every week and still have to turn them away because it has nobody available to make another visit. The way I see it, the biggest change for businesses over the next decade will be figuring out how to meet that kind of demand with a workforce that may be getting smaller at the same time.

Japan gives us an idea of what happens when the age of the customer changes. According to the Japanese government’s annual report on aging, people aged 65 or older made up 29.3% of the population in 2024. That change can show up in a business as ordinary as selling diapers. In 2024, Oji Holdings announced it would stop making baby diapers in Japan and focus its domestic diaper business on products for adults. It planned to continue its baby diaper business abroad. I think the decision says more about demographics than a single national population number does. The company could still sell the same kind of product in other countries, while what made sense to sell at home had changed.

Care is where I think the trend gets harder to manage. Japan’s health ministry estimates that the country will need about 2.72 million long-term care workers in fiscal year 2040, compared with roughly 2.15 million working in the field in fiscal year 2022. That is an estimate of how many workers will be needed, so it tells us the size of the hiring challenge. Imagine a family that can afford a few visits a week to help a parent stay at home. If the agency only has someone available on Tuesdays, what the family can actually buy is limited by who can show up. An increase in demand can mean a longer wait for care before it means more revenue for the agency.

I can see why falling birth rates get so much attention. Derek Thompson’s discussion of the fertility decline looks at why it is happening across countries that differ in how people live and what governments do. Noah Smith also argues that we still have little evidence of a reliable way to reverse it. Even if a government found a way to raise birth rates tomorrow, those children would take roughly two decades to reach the age when most people start working full time. A company that needs to hire in 2030 is dealing with people who are already alive. That is why I would put more weight on whether businesses can keep experienced employees than on whether a new family policy raises the birth rate.

The problem extends beyond Japan, although I would expect it to look different depending on where a business operates. The OECD projects that the working-age population across its member countries will fall by 8% by 2060. That average hides differences between countries, and it gives even less information about the town where a company is deciding whether to open a new location. An agency in a growing city might have enough applicants but lose workers to a hospital that pays more. In a town that younger people are leaving, the agency might struggle to find someone within driving distance. Those two businesses could face the same increase in demand and have very different ideas about how to handle it.

I expect technology to help most where employees spend a lot of time doing work that takes them away from the customer. In home care, a company could schedule visits so an aide spends less of the day driving back and forth across town. If that change saves enough time, the aide could take another visit without extending their shift. A company could also make it easier for someone to stay in the job by giving them a regular schedule instead of asking them to fill gaps at the last minute. I think that matters because an agency can keep hiring and still have the same number of workers if experienced aides keep leaving.

I would expect the economics to play out differently for a company like Oji. A manufacturer can shift investment toward a product that more people in its home market need, and it can keep serving younger customers from factories in other countries. A care provider has to find workers close enough to serve the people asking for help. That makes me more cautious about calling aging a good business opportunity across an entire industry. A growing customer base can justify adding a production line. For an agency paying staff to travel between homes, I would want to know what it costs to deliver one more hour of care before assuming more demand will translate into better results.

By the mid-2030s, I expect larger home care providers in older markets to concentrate new hiring in areas where they already have enough clients to give workers full and predictable shifts. A second office may sound like an easy way to add customers, but it could also spread the same workers across a wider area and leave them spending more time in the car. I think some providers will be able to grow faster by getting better at serving the neighborhoods they already cover. That could mean turning down a referral across town if it would make the rest of the route harder to cover. I’d look at whether they can accept more clients without raising staff turnover, because taking on more cases at the expense of losing experienced workers would make the growth hard to sustain.

What I take from Japan is that aging can create a bigger market before businesses have figured out how to serve it. I would take a company’s plan more seriously if it explained who would do the extra work and why those people would want to stay. For the home care agency getting a call from a family, the measure of growth is whether it can actually send someone to the house. That is where I expect businesses in aging countries to feel the difference between having more potential customers and being able to serve them.

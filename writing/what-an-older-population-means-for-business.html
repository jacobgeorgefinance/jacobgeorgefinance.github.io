What an Older Population Means for Business

Jacob George · November 2026

As populations age, more people can need care at the same time that fewer people are entering the workforce. For a home care agency, that can mean families are calling for help and the agency still has to turn them away because it cannot staff another visit. The shortage matters even when those families can afford to pay. Over the next decade, the biggest challenge for these businesses will be finding enough workers to provide the services people already need. Until they can do that, I expect rising demand to show up in longer waits before it shows up in more care.

Japan gives us an idea of what happens when the age of the customer changes. According to the Japanese government’s annual report on aging, people aged 65 or older made up 29.3% of the population in 2024. That change can show up in a business as ordinary as selling diapers. In 2024, Oji Holdings announced it would stop making baby diapers in Japan and focus its domestic diaper business on products for adults. It planned to continue its baby diaper business abroad. Oji could respond to an older customer base by changing what it made in Japan. Home care agencies face a different problem because every new customer needs time from a worker who lives near them.

Japan’s demand for long-term care shows how big that staffing problem could become. Japan’s health ministry estimates that the country will need about 2.72 million long-term care workers in fiscal year 2040, compared with roughly 2.15 million working in the field in fiscal year 2022. That means Japan will need about 570,000 more care workers than it had in 2022, even before accounting for the people who leave the field in the meantime. Imagine a family that can afford a few visits a week to help a parent stay at home. If the agency only has someone available on Tuesdays, what the family can actually buy is limited by who can show up. An increase in demand can mean a longer wait for care before it means more revenue for the agency.

I can see why falling birth rates get so much attention. Derek Thompson’s discussion of the fertility decline looks at why it is happening across countries that differ in how people live and what governments do. Noah Smith also argues that we still have little evidence of a reliable way to reverse it. Even if a government found a way to raise birth rates tomorrow, those children would take roughly two decades to reach the age when most people start working full time. A company that needs to hire in 2030 is dealing with people who are already alive. That is why I would put more weight on whether businesses can keep experienced employees than on whether a new family policy raises the birth rate.

The problem extends beyond Japan, although I would expect it to look different depending on where a business operates. The OECD projects that the working-age population across its member countries will fall by 8% by 2060. That average hides differences between countries, and it gives even less information about the town where a company is deciding whether to open a new location. An agency in a growing city might have enough applicants but lose workers to a hospital that pays more. In a town that younger people are leaving, the agency might struggle to find someone within driving distance. Those two businesses could face the same increase in demand and have very different ideas about how to handle it.

I expect technology to help most where employees spend a lot of time doing work that takes them away from the customer. In home care, a company could schedule visits so an aide spends less of the day driving back and forth across town. If that change saves enough time, the aide could take another visit without extending their shift. A company could also make it easier for someone to stay in the job by giving them a regular schedule instead of asking them to fill gaps at the last minute. I think that matters because an agency can keep hiring and still have the same number of workers if experienced aides keep leaving.

A shortage also changes who ends up paying for care. An agency might be able to hire more aides by offering higher wages, but only if it can afford to pay them. If it passes that cost on to families, some will have to buy fewer visits. If a family cannot find an aide at all, an adult child might cut back on paid work to look after a parent. That is how the shortage can reach businesses outside the care industry. An employer may lose hours from experienced workers because those workers have to make up for care their families cannot get.

By the mid-2030s, I expect larger home care providers in older markets to concentrate new hiring in areas where they already have enough clients to give workers full and predictable shifts. A second office may sound like an easy way to add customers, but it could also spread the same workers across a wider area and leave them spending more time in the car. I think some providers will be able to grow faster by getting better at serving the neighborhoods they already cover. That could mean turning down a referral across town if it would make the rest of the route harder to cover. I’d look at whether they can accept more clients without raising staff turnover, because taking on more cases at the expense of losing experienced workers would make the growth hard to sustain.

The part I would watch is whether the number of hours of care that agencies can provide keeps up with what families need. A longer waitlist can look like a bigger market from the outside. For the agency, it means there are more people asking for a service it already struggles to provide. Better scheduling can help an aide fit in another visit, but it can only go so far if too few people stay in the job. That is why the workforce matters more to my prediction than the number of potential customers.

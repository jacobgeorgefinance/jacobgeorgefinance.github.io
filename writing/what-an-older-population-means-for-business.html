What an Older Population Means for Business

Jacob George · November 2026

If I ran a home care agency and could hire only one more aide, I’d rather send that person to five homes in the same neighborhood than to five homes spread across a county. The families in both places could need the same amount of help, yet the agency gets more visits out of the same shift when the homes are close together. As the population ages and aides get harder to hire, I expect providers to fill out their routes in places they already serve. That will leave families in more spread-out towns with fewer options even as more people there need care.

Japan gives us an idea of what happens when the age of the customer changes. According to the Japanese government’s annual report on aging, people aged 65 or older made up 29.3% of the population in 2024. That change can show up in a business as ordinary as selling diapers. In 2024, Oji Holdings announced it would stop making baby diapers in Japan and focus its domestic diaper business on products for adults. It planned to continue its baby diaper business abroad. Oji could respond to an older customer base by changing what it made in Japan. Home care agencies face a different problem because every new customer needs time from a worker who lives near them.

Japan’s demand for long-term care shows how big that staffing problem could become. Japan’s health ministry estimates that the country will need about 2.72 million long-term care workers in fiscal year 2040, compared with roughly 2.15 million working in the field in fiscal year 2022. That means Japan will need about 570,000 more care workers than it had in 2022, even before accounting for the people who leave the field in the meantime. Imagine a family that can afford a few visits a week to help a parent stay at home. If the agency only has someone available on Tuesdays, what the family can actually buy is limited by who can show up. An increase in demand can mean a longer wait for care before it means more revenue for the agency.

I can see why falling birth rates get so much attention. Derek Thompson’s discussion of the fertility decline looks at why it is happening across countries that differ in how people live and what governments do. Noah Smith also argues that we still have little evidence of a reliable way to reverse it. Even if a government found a way to raise birth rates tomorrow, those children would take roughly two decades to reach the age when most people start working full time. A company that needs to hire in 2030 is dealing with people who are already alive. That is why I would put more weight on whether businesses can keep experienced employees than on whether a new family policy raises the birth rate.

The problem extends beyond Japan, although I would expect it to look different depending on where a business operates. The OECD projects that the working-age population across its member countries will fall by 8% by 2060. The average tells me why hiring could get harder across many countries, but the effect will depend on the town. An agency in a growing city might lose applicants to a hospital that pays more, but each aide it does hire can cover several nearby households. In a town that younger people are leaving, finding an aide may take longer. Even after the agency fills the position, it may take that worker much longer to get to each house.

A review of U.S. home health agencies found that longer distances between patients raised travel costs and limited how many visits rural providers could make in a day. Think of two aides who each visit five households. If one has a 15-minute drive between homes and the other has a 45-minute drive, the second aide spends two more hours on the road over the course of those visits. Better scheduling might help the agency cut some of that time. If the next person who needs care lives miles from everyone else on the route, there is only so much a better schedule can do. That gives the agency a reason to take the closer referral first.

That choice shifts some of the cost of care onto families who live farther away. An agency might be able to hire more aides by offering higher wages, but only if it can afford to pay them. If it passes that cost on to families, some will have to buy fewer visits. If a family cannot find an aide at all, an adult child might cut back on paid work to look after a parent. That is how the shortage can reach businesses outside the care industry. An employer may lose hours from experienced workers because those workers have to make up for care their families cannot get.

By the mid-2030s, I expect larger home care providers to take on more clients in areas where they already have a regular route. Picture an agency choosing between opening another office in a nearby town and hiring an extra aide for a route it already covers. The existing route gives the aide a steadier shift because there are enough visits close together to fill it. There may still be a case for offering care farther out if the agency gets paid enough to cover the longer drives. If every visit pays roughly the same, I’d expect the agency to keep expanding in the neighborhoods it can cover with the workers it has. A county could pay a provider more for visits on a route that would otherwise go unserved. Nearby towns could also share an aide on set days so several visits make the drive worthwhile. In either case, somebody still has to pay for the time that worker spends in the car. That cost matters more as providers have fewer hours of staff time to go around.

I’d judge this prediction by whether providers widen the areas they serve as they grow. If an agency takes on more clients in town while turning away referrals from the surrounding county, growth in its client count can hide a loss of access for some families. Where that happens, paying for a visit only gets a family so far if nobody is willing to make the drive. That’s the difference I expect aging to expose. Two families can need the same care and have the same ability to pay, but one can live too far from the rest of the route to get it.

Where Digital Money Will Make a Difference

Jacob George · November 2026

I think the biggest change in finance over the next decade will come from making it easier to send money across borders. If I buy coffee in New York, I can tap my phone and see the payment right away. Sending money to someone in another country can still mean checking exchange rates, paying a fee and waiting to see when it shows up. That feels like a better problem for fintech to solve than getting people to buy things with Bitcoin. I expect stablecoins to help in some international transfers, but for most people the change will probably show up in the payment app they already use.

Kenya is a good example of how much a better way to pay can matter. According to the World Bank’s Global Findex, about 42% of Kenyan adults had a financial account in 2011. By 2024, about 90% had an account at a financial institution or with a mobile-money provider, and about 88% had a mobile-money account. Those last two groups overlap. What I find interesting about M-PESA is that someone could bring cash to a local agent and turn it into a balance they could send by phone. The person receiving it could withdraw cash from an agent on their end. Once that system was in place, people had a reason to use it even if they had never visited a bank branch.

I see a similar opportunity in international payments. In the third quarter of 2025, the World Bank put the average cost of sending $200 abroad at 6.36%, or about $12.72. That adds up for someone sending money home every month. A dollar stablecoin is a token designed to stay worth one dollar. A payment company could take dollars from a customer in the U.S., send a stablecoin to its overseas partner and have that partner pay the recipient in local currency. The transfer between the companies could go through on a weekend. The person receiving the money could still get it in the account they already use for groceries and bills.

That last step is also where I think the claims about stablecoins get ahead of reality. A transfer can arrive on a blockchain in seconds, but the recipient still needs a way to turn it into money they can spend. If the local partner charges a high fee or gives the customer a bad exchange rate, faster settlement may do very little for them. The Federal Reserve’s analysis of cross-border stablecoin payments makes the point that getting into and out of a stablecoin can add its own costs. I’d judge a service by how much local currency reaches the recipient and when they can actually use it. The speed of the transfer in the middle tells only part of the story.

Bitcoin has a harder time solving that problem because its price can move on its own. The Coinbase price series published by the St. Louis Fed shows Bitcoin trading above $60,000 in November 2021 and below $20,000 a year later. If I’m paying a supplier, I want both of us to know what the payment is worth in the currency we use to pay our bills. A stablecoin is designed to avoid that swing, but its value still depends on whether the issuer can give people their dollars back. The Bank for International Settlements points out that a stablecoin is a liability of a specific issuer. To me, that means the software can change how a payment moves while the question of who stands behind the money still matters.

I think the first changes most people encounter will happen behind the scenes. Visa announced in December 2025 that two U.S. banks had started settling with it in USDC. A customer could still use their Visa card the way they always did while a bank had more flexibility to settle with Visa outside the regular workweek. That gives me a clearer picture of what adoption might look like. People already have cards and bank accounts, and they have little reason to learn how to manage a crypto wallet just to buy lunch. Banks and payment companies, on the other hand, have a reason to try a system that lets them move funds on a Sunday. A bank that needs the money on Monday morning could move it before its staff comes back to work.

Tokenization could change how those institutions work together, too. In Project Agorá, a group of central banks and financial institutions built a prototype that put digital versions of bank deposits and central bank reserves on a shared platform. It showed that both sides of a cross-currency payment could settle together. If a bank is exchanging dollars for euros, that means it could release the dollars only when the euros are ready to move at the same time. A similar idea makes sense for a business paying for a shipment. The payment could release after delivery is confirmed, although the buyer and seller would still need to agree on what happens if the shipment arrives damaged or the delivery record is wrong.

By the mid-2030s, I expect more banks and payment companies to give customers a price and a delivery time before they send money abroad, even on weekends. Some companies will use stablecoins for the international leg, while others will use faster bank networks or tokenized deposits. I’d expect those options to compete hardest on routes where customers still pay high fees or regularly have to wait several days. The companies that already reach people on both ends of the transfer will have an advantage, since a faster transfer means little if the recipient has to travel to find somewhere to cash it out. I’d also expect businesses that pay overseas suppliers every week to switch sooner than people who send money abroad once a year. A delay that keeps happening is worth paying to fix.

I think blockchain can improve part of what financial intermediaries do. The examples that persuade me are the ones where a bank can settle on a weekend, a business can tie a payment to delivery or a family can receive more of what someone sent. For a person transferring $200 home, the improvement is easy to recognize. They know what it costs, the money arrives when the app says it will, and the person on the other end can spend it. That’s the change I think will make digital money matter to people who have never bought a cryptocurrency.

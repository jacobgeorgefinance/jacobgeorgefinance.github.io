Where Digital Money Will Make a Difference

Jacob George · November 2026

If I send money abroad on a Friday, the payment can leave my account that day while the person I sent it to still has to wait until Monday. I think closing that gap will be one of the most important changes in finance over the next decade, especially for people who send part of every paycheck home. Stablecoins could help by letting payment companies move dollars to their partners over the weekend. I expect that to happen inside the apps people already use, because a company can change how it sends the money without asking its customers to learn a new way to pay.

Kenya is a good example of how much a better way to pay can matter. According to the World Bank’s Global Findex, about 42% of Kenyan adults had a financial account in 2011. By 2024, about 90% had some kind of financial account, with nearly as many, about 88% of adults, using an account with a mobile-money provider. What I find interesting about M-PESA is that someone could bring cash to a local agent and turn it into a balance they could send by phone. The person receiving it could withdraw cash from an agent on their end. Once that system was in place, people had a reason to use it even if they had never visited a bank branch.

I see a similar opportunity in international payments. In the third quarter of 2025, the World Bank put the average cost of sending $200 abroad at 6.36%, or about $12.72. For someone who sends money home every month, a fee like that quickly adds up. The customer could still pay the transfer company in dollars, while the company pays its overseas partner with stablecoins designed to hold their dollar value. The partner would handle the conversion to local currency and deposit the money into the recipient’s usual account.

That last step is also where I think the claims about stablecoins get ahead of reality. A transfer can arrive on a blockchain in seconds, but the recipient still needs a way to turn it into money they can spend. A bad exchange rate from the local partner could eat up the savings from a faster transfer. The Federal Reserve’s analysis of cross-border stablecoin payments makes the point that getting into and out of a stablecoin can add its own costs. What I’d check is how much local currency actually shows up in the recipient’s account, since the company can move the payment faster without improving the deal for the person receiving it.

Bitcoin has a harder time solving that problem because its price can move on its own. The Coinbase price series published by the St. Louis Fed shows Bitcoin trading above $60,000 in November 2021 and below $20,000 a year later. If I’m paying a supplier, I want both of us to know what the payment is worth in the currency we use to pay our bills. A stablecoin is designed to avoid that swing, but its value still depends on whether the issuer can give people their dollars back. The Bank for International Settlements points out that a stablecoin is a liability of a specific issuer. To me, that means the software can change how a payment moves while the question of who stands behind the money still matters.

I think the first changes most people encounter will happen behind the scenes. Visa announced in December 2025 that two U.S. banks had started settling with it in USDC. A customer could still use their Visa card the way they always did while a bank had more flexibility to settle with Visa outside the regular workweek. That’s how I expect most people to encounter stablecoins, since someone who already pays by card has little reason to learn how to manage a crypto wallet just to buy lunch. A bank could use Sunday settlement to get the cash it needs for Monday morning, when payments are due and its usual workweek has barely begun. That could reduce how much cash the bank has to leave idle over the weekend.

Tokenization could change how those institutions work together, too. In Project Agorá, a group of central banks and financial institutions built a prototype that put digital versions of bank deposits and central bank reserves on a shared platform. It showed that both sides of a cross-currency payment could settle together. If a bank is exchanging dollars for euros, that means it could release the dollars only when the euros are ready to move at the same time. A business could use a similar setup to release payment when a shipment is marked as delivered, as long as the contract gives the buyer a way to dispute the payment if the goods arrive damaged.

By the mid-2030s, I expect more banks and payment companies to give customers a price and a delivery time before they send money abroad, even on weekends. Stablecoins could carry part of the transfer in places where banks still take days to move money. On routes already served by fast bank networks, I’d expect a new service to win customers by offering a better price. The companies that already reach people on both ends of the transfer will have an advantage, since a faster transfer means little if the recipient has to travel to find somewhere to cash it out. I’d expect businesses that pay overseas suppliers every week to switch sooner, because a delay that holds up the next order every week is worth paying to fix. Think of an importer that sends money on Wednesday because its supplier needs to be paid before shipping on Monday. If the payment company can reliably deliver the money over the weekend, the importer could hold onto the cash until Friday. That gives a small business a little more room to cover other expenses before the money leaves its account.

I’ll be convinced stablecoins are changing finance when a company can use them to make an ordinary $200 transfer cheaper for the person sending it. If the company saves money moving funds overseas but keeps charging the same fee, the improvement belongs to the company. I expect the savings to reach customers first on routes where several services already compete to send money to the same place. A service that can deliver more of each paycheck to the family receiving it gives customers a reason to switch that they can see the first time they use it.

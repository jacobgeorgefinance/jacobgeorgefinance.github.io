The Future of Work

Jacob George · November 2026

I think the future of work will still revolve around having a regular job, but the way people do that job will become more flexible. I expect more office workers to split their week between home and the office, and more people to pick up freelance work alongside a steady paycheck. How much freedom that gives someone depends on what they need from the job. A student who delivers food on weekends can take time off during exams, while someone who counts on those deliveries to cover rent has to plan around when orders come in.

The size of the gig economy depends on who counts as a gig worker. A 2016 McKinsey report estimated that 68 million people in the U.S. did some form of independent work, and more than half of them did it for extra income. The second number matters more for my prediction because it tells us how people were using gig work. A steady paycheck gives someone room to turn down a project that pays too little. For a student, the appeal might be picking up work over winter break and having the option to stop once classes resume. I think that’s how a lot of people will keep using gigs, especially when their schedule or expenses change.

For a business, hiring a freelancer makes sense when there’s a clear end to the project. A company that needs a new website can pay someone to build it, then decide whether it has enough work to keep that person on. Once the site goes live, the freelancer has to find another client or go without that income. In the U.S., where a job can also provide health insurance and retirement benefits, a freelancer has to think about more than the rate a client offers. I think gig work will keep growing for jobs a company can break into individual projects. When there’s enough work to keep somebody busy every week, I think employers will still have a reason to hire someone who knows the business and can stay with it. That matters when customers expect to talk to someone who remembers what went wrong with their last order.

I could also see AI changing which gigs are worth paying for. If a client can use AI to write a basic product description, they might stop hiring someone to write each one from scratch. They might still pay someone who knows the product to check whether the draft is accurate and sounds like the company. That could make it harder to get started as a freelancer, since a simple writing assignment can give someone their first paid sample to show the next client. The International Labour Organization expects AI to change what people do in many jobs. I’m less sure what will replace those beginner assignments, especially if clients start looking for people who already know enough to catch mistakes in an AI draft.

Working from home gives people a different kind of flexibility because they can save time on a commute and still get a regular paycheck. In a survey from early 2023, about 29% of full-time U.S. employees worked a hybrid schedule and about 12% worked fully from home. A review of remote work research found little difference in productivity for hybrid workers, while fully remote work was associated with lower productivity. In a study of software engineers, people got more feedback when they sat near their teammates, and the biggest gains were for younger and newer employees. That helps explain why I expect hybrid schedules to last. People can get feedback in person when they need it and save the commute on days when they already know what they have to finish.

As someone interested in finance, I’d want more time in the office when I’m starting out. If I were building a financial model for the first time, I’d probably have questions about which assumptions to use and how much detail my team expects. I’d feel more comfortable asking someone sitting nearby than setting up a call every time I got stuck. Once I knew how the team worked, I could see myself getting more done at home on a day when I just needed a few hours to finish the model. I’d expect teams to set a couple of shared office days for that reason. If everyone chooses their days separately, a new analyst could spend an hour commuting and still have to ask the same questions over Zoom.

In New York, an office deli might have a line out the door on the days when teams come in together and a much slower lunch on Friday. That makes it harder for the deli to know how much food to prep for lunch. If fewer people need to be in the city every day, some will probably decide they can live farther away. For someone like me who’s trying to get into finance, living in the city means I can meet someone for coffee after class, even if the only time they have free is on one of their two days in the office.

Where a company looks for employees could change too. If a New York firm only brings its team together once a month, it could interview someone in Philadelphia who can take the train in for those meetings. A firm that needs people in three days a week would still have to find someone close enough to make the trip regularly. I think that difference will matter when people apply for jobs. Two listings can both say “hybrid” and give people very different ideas of where they can afford to live.

The kind of flexibility people can get depends on the job. A nurse who needs to be on the hospital floor might get more out of being able to swap a Saturday shift or seeing the next month’s schedule early enough to make plans.

Over the next few years, I think more employers will get specific about when they need people in the office and when they need to hire someone full time. I’d expect to see teams bring new hires in more often at the start, then give them more say over their schedules once they know the job. I’d also expect people to keep picking up gigs when they need extra money. If I were choosing my first job, I’d probably care more about who would teach me and how often I’d get to work with them than how many days I could stay home.

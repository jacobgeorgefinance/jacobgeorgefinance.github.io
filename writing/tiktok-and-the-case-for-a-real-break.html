TikTok and the Case for a Real Break

Jacob George · November 2026

Social media companies are facing a shift in what governments expect them to take responsibility for. For a long time, much of the argument was about which posts platforms should remove. Regulators are now also asking how apps decide what people see and how they use the information they collect. I think the strongest case for changing the apps is that their design makes it unnecessarily hard to stop scrolling. If I open TikTok to see a video someone sent me, I can end up on the For You feed without a natural moment to decide whether I want to keep watching. Over the next three years, I expect TikTok to make teenagers in the EU wait through a break from the feed, because optional reminders leave that decision to the person the app is trying to keep on it.

In February 2026, the European Commission singled out infinite scroll, autoplay and personalized recommendations in its preliminary findings on TikTok. It also said TikTok had overlooked how often younger users opened the app and how much time they spent on it at night. I think those details matter more than a daily average. Two people can each spend an hour on TikTok, but one might watch a few clips at lunch and the other might scroll for an hour straight after getting into bed. A daily limit treats those two hours the same. A break during a long session targets the second situation, where the design of the feed makes it easy to stay on much longer than planned.

What I think gets overstated is how much of the decline in teen mental health we can explain with social media alone. In internal Instagram research reported by The Wall Street Journal, 32% of the teen girls surveyed said Instagram made them feel worse when they were already feeling bad about their bodies. That gives me a reason to take the effect seriously for those girls. It would be a stretch to use the same finding to explain why an entire generation is struggling. Jean Twenge and Heejung Park also found that fewer U.S. teens were getting behind the wheel or working for pay. A teenager who can’t drive and doesn’t have a job may have fewer chances to spend time away from home. Several things about growing up changed over that period, so I’d be careful about giving any one of them all the blame.

I also disagree with Kelsey Piper’s argument against age-gating to the extent that it treats the privacy costs as a reason to give up on serious age restrictions. I would be uncomfortable uploading a driver’s license directly to a social media company, especially if it could hold onto it. The EU’s age verification blueprint shows how a platform could receive proof that a person meets an age threshold without receiving their name or exact birthday. I’d support keeping kids under 16 off apps with feeds like TikTok’s if the age check worked that way. The details matter because a rule meant to protect kids can create a new problem if it gives platforms more personal information than they need.

For teens old enough to have an account, my prediction is that TikTok will temporarily freeze the For You feed after a long stretch of continuous scrolling in the EU by late 2029. I could see it starting with something like a five-minute pause after 30 minutes of uninterrupted use. During that break, the next video would stay unavailable even if the user closed and reopened the app. TikTok has already introduced a default 60-minute daily limit that teens can extend by entering a passcode. It has also introduced a meditation prompt at 10 p.m. for teens. A pause you actually have to wait through would go further because there would be time to put the phone down before the next clip starts.

I expect TikTok to try this in Europe first because the EU has already gotten the company to change its product there. In 2024, TikTok agreed to permanently drop the TikTok Lite rewards program in the EU after the Commission opened proceedings. Users had been able to earn points for activities like watching videos. In its February 2026 findings on the main app, the Commission raised effective screen-time breaks as one possible response. The earlier case gives me a reason to think TikTok would agree to a change in Europe while keeping the regular feed elsewhere. The company would have to give up some chances to show ads when a teen would otherwise keep watching, so I’d expect it to start where it’s under the most pressure to act.

The Commission’s November findings also point to a question about what the feed shows after a break. TikTok can keep recommending similar videos after a user watches one about body image. The Digital Services Act addresses more than that one feature. It requires the biggest platforms to offer a feed option that is not based on profiling, and it gives users a way to challenge some moderation decisions. That matters when a platform removes a post or recommends a piece of content, since both decisions shape what users actually get to see.

The harder part is figuring out whether the pause actually helps. If teens hit the break and immediately switch to Instagram Reels or YouTube Shorts, TikTok might report less time on its app even though those users are still watching videos at the same hour. I’d want researchers to see whether teens who hit a late-night pause actually went to sleep earlier. A company can tell when somebody closes its own app, but it would take independent research to understand what that person does next without asking TikTok to track them across their phone. A study could ask teens to record their bedtimes and voluntarily share their phone’s screen-time totals.

The clearest sign that the backlash has changed TikTok will be what happens when a teen reaches the limit. A reminder with a button that sends them right back to the feed leaves the decision exactly where it was before. I expect the European version to make the next video wait, long enough for someone to decide if they still want to watch it. At night, that pause could be enough time to put the phone down for good.
